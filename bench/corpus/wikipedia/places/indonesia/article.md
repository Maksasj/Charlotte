# Indonesia

Indonesia, officially the Republic of Indonesia, is a country in Southeast Asia and Oceania, between the Indian and Pacific oceans. Comprising more than 17,000 islands—including Sumatra, Java, Sulawesi, and parts of Borneo and New Guinea—it is the world's largest archipelagic state and the 14th-largest country by area. Indonesia has extensive tropical forests and marine ecosystems that support some of the world's highest levels of biodiversity.

The Indonesian archipelago has been inhabited since prehistoric times, with evidence of early human presence including hominin fossils, cave paintings and stone monuments. By the first millennium BCE, it had become connected to regional and international maritime trade networks. Hindu and Buddhist kingdoms developed by the early centuries CE, followed by the spread of Islam and the growth of Islamic states from the late medieval period. European powers later competed to monopolise trade in the Spice Islands of Maluku during the Age of Discovery. Dutch commercial and colonial control expanded from the 17th century and extended over most of the archipelago by the early 20th century, before Indonesia proclaimed its independence in the aftermath of World War II.

Since independence, Indonesia has experienced separatist conflicts, corruption, political upheaval and natural disasters, alongside democratisation and rapid economic growth. The country today is a presidential republic with an elected legislature and consists of 38 provinces, some of which enjoy greater autonomy than others. Home to over 290 million people, Indonesia ranks fourth in the world by population and has the largest Muslim population of any country. More than half of Indonesians live on Java, the most heavily populated island in the world, while the capital Jakarta is listed as the world's most populous city by the United Nations.

Indonesia has hundreds of ethnic groups and more than 700 languages, as well as diverse regional cultures. This diversity is reflected in the national motto, Bhinneka Tunggal Ika, meaning "unity in diversity". Indonesian, a standardised variety of Malay, is the official and national language and is widely used for communication between speakers of different local languages. Indonesia has the largest economy in Southeast Asia, with services and industry generating most output and economic activity concentrated on Java. Sea and air transport are the primary means of connecting the country's many islands. Indonesia is a middle power active in regional and global affairs and is a member of the United Nations, G20, the Non-Aligned Movement, ASEAN, and the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation.

## Etymology

The name Indonesia is usually explained as a combination of India and the Greek word nesos, together meaning 'Indian islands'. In 1850, George Windsor Earl, an English ethnologist, proposed the terms Indunesians and Malayunesians for the inhabitants of the "Indian Archipelago or Malay Archipelago", preferring the latter because he considered Indunesians too general. Earl's colleague James Richardson Logan adopted Indonesia as a geographical term for the Indian Archipelago.

German ethnographer Adolf Bastian from the University of Berlin helped popularise the name among scholars by using it in the title of his five-volume work Indonesia, or the Islands of the Malay Archipelago (German: Indonesien oder die Inseln des Malayischen Archipel), published between 1884 and 1894. Dutch scholars nevertheless tended to use Malay Archipelago (Dutch: Maleische Archipel) or the Netherlands East Indies (Nederlandsch Oost Indië).

After 1900, Indonesia became increasingly common in academic and public usage, and Indonesian nationalist organisations adopted it as an expression of national unity and freedom from colonial rule. Among its early Indonesian promoters was Soewardi Soerjaningrat, later known as Ki Hajar Dewantara, who established the Indonesische Persbureau in The Hague in November 1918, a press bureau that used Indonesia in its name.

### Prehistory, early states, and Islamisation

The island of Java has yielded numerous hominin fossils generally attributed to Homo erectus, including remains discovered by Eugène Dubois at Trinil. Stone artefacts on Flores show that hominins had reached the island by at least 1.02 million years ago. Homo sapiens had dispersed into Southeast Asia by at least 50,000 years ago. In Sulawesi, a cave painting showing human-like figures and a pig has been dated to a minimum age of 51,200 years. Flores also yielded remains of the small-bodied hominin Homo floresiensis. Megalithic traditions later developed at different times across the archipelago, and stone monuments were used for burials and to honour or commemorate ancestors.

Beginning in the third millennium BCE, Austronesian-speaking communities moved south from Taiwan through the Philippines and into the Indonesian archipelago, where related languages spread across most of the islands. Settlement and subsistence practices varied across the islands, with communities combining fishing, hunting, gathering and cultivation. From around 500 BCE, regional trading networks increasingly connected the archipelago with South and East Asia. Goods, knowledge and technologies circulated through these networks, and, during the early centuries CE, some coastal communities in Borneo, Sumatra, and Java developed into trading ports. Indian merchants and religious specialists brought Hindu and Buddhist practices to the archipelago, and some local rulers adopted Indian royal titles and used Sanskrit in inscriptions.

Srivijaya, founded in southeastern Sumatra in the late 7th century, grew as a maritime trading polity and a centre of Buddhist learning and religious networks. In central Java, rulers supported the construction of major religious monuments between the 8th and 10th centuries, including the Buddhist Borobudur and Hindu Prambanan temple complexes. Majapahit emerged in eastern Java in the late 13th century after Raden Wijaya used a Mongol expedition to defeat his rivals and then forced the Mongols to withdraw. Its rulers claimed authority over ports across the archipelago, but beyond Java much of this influence depended on tributary relationships and ceremonial recognition rather than direct rule.

By the late 13th century, Muslim rule had been established in northern Sumatra. Marco Polo reported that Perlak was a Muslim town during his visit in 1292, and a royal tomb at Pasai dated 1297 records a Muslim ruler there. The sultanate of Samudera Pasai became an important pepper port and a major centre for Islamic learning. Islam spread further through maritime trading networks, with Muslim communities becoming established at ports along Java's north coast and elsewhere in the archipelago. Majapahit declined during the 15th century, and Demak emerged as an independent north-coast sultanate and an important centre of Javanese Islam. Javanese traditions credit the Wali Sanga, a group of nine saints, with spreading Islam in Java, but historical evidence about their lives and activities is limited. Islamic practice there also incorporated elements of older Javanese culture.

### European contact and colonial rule

Before European arrival, spices from Maluku were already traded through Asian maritime networks in which Javanese and other regional merchants participated. The island sultanates of Ternate and Tidore became regional powers during the 15th century. The Portuguese captured the trading port of Malacca in 1511 and reached Maluku the following year, where they sought control of the spice trade. Dutch expeditions followed in the 1590s, and in 1602 competing Dutch companies were merged into the Dutch East India Company (Vereenigde Oost-Indische Compagnie, VOC). Its charter authorised it to wage war, build fortresses, and conclude treaties in Asia.

In the 17th century, local states continued to expand, including the Mataram Sultanate across much of Java and the Gowa Sultanate on the southern peninsula of Sulawesi, where the port of Makassar became a major trading centre. In 1619, the VOC captured Jayakarta (present-day Jakarta) and renamed it Batavia. It developed as an administrative and commercial centre and attracted Chinese migrants and others from the archipelago. Over the next two centuries, the VOC imposed monopolies to control the spice trade and expanded its influence in Java through military campaigns and intervention in local disputes. The VOC was dissolved in 1800 after going bankrupt, and its possessions passed to the Dutch government. During the Napoleonic Wars, British forces captured Java in 1811, but the island was returned to the Dutch in 1816.

In the 19th century, Dutch power expanded across the archipelago, meeting resistance in the Java War and in Sumatra during the Padri War and Aceh War. The Dutch established an outpost in western New Guinea in 1828 but abandoned it in 1836 and began permanent occupation only after 1898. In Bali, a series of Dutch campaigns against the independent kingdoms began in 1846 and culminated in 1908. By the early 20th century, military campaigns and boundary agreements had established Dutch control over a territory that largely corresponded to present-day Indonesia, known then as the Netherlands Indies. The colonial administration ruled several territories directly and governed others through local states whose rulers retained limited authority under Dutch supremacy.

In Java, the Dutch had introduced the Cultivation System in 1830, requiring villagers to devote part of their land and labour to government-designated crops as payment for land tax. Criticism of the system helped establish colonial policy as a subject of public debate in the Netherlands. In 1901, the Dutch government adopted the Ethical Policy, which aimed to improve welfare in the colony. The policy established primary schools teaching in local languages and expanded irrigation and other public services. Western-style schooling also expanded, but it reached only a small minority of the population. In 1905, the colonial government also began resettling people from Java to less densely populated islands.

### Emergence of Indonesia and independence

Colonial education, despite its limited reach, helped produce an educated elite who became active in the emerging nationalist movements. Several political organisations were founded during this period, including the Javanese cultural organisation Budi Utomo and the mass Islamic movement Sarekat Islam, followed by the Communist Party of Indonesia (PKI) and the Indonesian National Party. During the 1920s, a shared national identity began to develop through youth associations and a growing body of literature in Malay, which for centuries had been used for communication between different ethnic groups. Intellectuals increasingly called the language Bahasa Indonesia and made it a common medium for the emerging nation. The Youth Pledge in 1928 recognised it as the "language of unity", alongside the ideals of one homeland and one nation, Indonesia.

The Great Depression reached the colony in the early 1930s, bringing severe economic hardship and increasing fears among colonial authorities that discontent could strengthen radical nationalist movements. Restrictions on nationalist activity became stricter, and by the mid-1930s, Sukarno, Mohammad Hatta, Sutan Sjahrir, and other nationalist leaders who rejected cooperation with colonial authorities had either been imprisoned or exiled. In 1942, Dutch colonial rule collapsed following the Japanese conquest of the colony during the Pacific War.

Japanese occupation imposed martial law, banned political parties and mobilised Indonesians through Japanese-sponsored civil and quasi-military organisations. Wartime shortages and forced labour caused widespread hardship, but the ban on the Dutch language strengthened the public use of the Indonesian language. Japanese-organised military units, including PETA on Java and the Giyugun on Sumatra, trained tens of thousands of Indonesians. On 7 August 1945, Japanese authorities authorised preparations for an Indonesian state planned for September, which would remain under Japanese military supervision.

However, Japan surrendered to the Allies on 15 August 1945. Two days later, Sukarno proclaimed Indonesian independence, with Hatta co-signing the declaration. They were named president and vice-president on 18 August. Dutch attempts to restore colonial rule led to a four-year military and diplomatic struggle. International opposition to the Netherlands intensified after Dutch forces captured Yogyakarta in a December 1948 offensive, and the United States subsequently pressed the Dutch into negotiations recommended by the United Nations. On 27 December 1949, the Netherlands transferred sovereignty to the federal United States of Indonesia, except over western New Guinea. The federal states soon joined the Republic, and Indonesia was proclaimed a unitary state on 17 August 1950.

### Sukarno era and Suharto's New Order

Indonesia adopted a parliamentary system of government under the 1950 provisional constitution. The early republic faced armed opposition from the Darul Islam rebellion, whose supporters aimed to establish an Islamic state. The dispute over western New Guinea strained relations between Indonesia and the Netherlands, which refused to negotiate its transfer to Indonesian control. In February 1958, dissidents in West Sumatra proclaimed a rival government before being joined by rebels in Sulawesi. They were seeking reforms to the republic rather than separation but were defeated by the central government. After the Constituent Assembly failed to secure the required majority for a permanent constitution, Sukarno dissolved it by decree in 1959 and restored the 1945 Constitution.

This established Guided Democracy, a system characterised by dominant presidential authority, less regional autonomy and a reduced role for political parties. Under this system, Sukarno expanded the military's political role while supporting the growing PKI as a counterweight. He also promoted state enterprises and import substitution, even as foreign investment declined, inflation soared and shortages of consumer goods worsened by the mid-1960s. The western New Guinea dispute culminated in the US-mediated New York Agreement in 1962, and Indonesia administered the territory in May 1963. In January 1963, Sukarno pursued Konfrontasi, opposing the formation of Malaysia through diplomatic pressure and armed incursions into its territory.

After military officers belonging to the 30 September Movement killed six generals in 1965, forces of Major General Suharto took control of Jakarta, and the military presented the movement as an attempted coup d'etat by the PKI. The ensuing anti-communist campaign spread across the country, with army units working alongside or directing civilian groups. At least 500,000 people were killed over a few months, and hundreds of thousands of others were detained. The campaign destroyed the PKI and shifted power to the military. In March 1966, Sukarno signed an order known as Supersemar, authorising Suharto to restore order and giving him de facto control over key executive and security powers. Suharto became president in 1968, and Sukarno died in 1970.

Suharto's "New Order" restricted political organisation, embedded the military in government, and used Golkar, an organisation backed by the military and civil service, to dominate elections in a tightly controlled party system. The Act of Free Choice in 1969 formally incorporated western New Guinea into Indonesia, but just over a thousand selected community leaders took part in it amid reports of military intimidation. Many Papuans opposed this integration and have waged an armed resistance since then. In 1975, Indonesia invaded Portuguese Timor and annexed it the following year. Hasan di Tiro founded the Free Aceh Movement in 1976 and proclaimed Aceh's independence from Indonesia. The insurgency there was largely suppressed by 1979 but revived in 1989 after the return of fighters trained in Libya, prompting a military campaign lasting a decade.

The New Order encouraged renewed foreign investment and sought foreign aid while pursuing industrialisation through state development plans. From the 1970s, the regime greatly expanded the transmigration programme, resettling families from Java to other islands. Manufacturing expanded and the number of people below the official poverty line fell substantially by the mid-1990s. At the same time, large business groups connected to Suharto's family and close associates accumulated wealth through state patronage and political protection. The Asian financial crisis of 1997–98 caused a sharp economic downturn, followed by protests and riots in May 1998, culminating in Suharto's resignation.

### Reformasi and contemporary Indonesia

The post-Suharto transition was marked by civil unrest, including the conflict between Christians and Muslims in Maluku and violence against Madurese communities in Kalimantan. The ensuing period of political reform, known as Reformasi, brought constitutional amendments, competitive elections, and a transfer of authority from the central government to local administrations. President B. J. Habibie relaxed restrictions on the press and political parties and oversaw the 1999 general election and the independence referendum in East Timor. Decentralisation began in 2001 under President Abdurrahman Wahid, while constitutional amendments were completed in 2002 during Megawati Sukarnoputri's presidency. One of the amendments paved the way for direct presidential elections.

The military lost the seats once reserved for its representatives in parliament, but it continued to influence government. New democratic institutions were established, but powerful political and business elites continued to exercise influence through patronage networks. Former general Susilo Bambang Yudhoyono won the first direct presidential election in 2004. His administration reached a peace agreement with Aceh separatists in 2005 after the previous year's Indian Ocean tsunami brought more international attention to the province. After a series of bombings by Islamist militant groups in the 2000s, large-scale attacks declined by the early 2010s, though smaller attacks continued.

Joko Widodo, popularly called Jokowi, was elected president in 2014 and prioritised infrastructure and economic development, which he linked to improving welfare. Indonesia remained an electoral democracy, but the quality of its democracy declined amid weakened parliamentary opposition and oversight, curbs on dissent, reduced independence of the anti-corruption commission, and Jokowi's intervention in the 2024 election. Under his successor Prabowo Subianto, the government pursued large-scale public spending programmes and revised the law governing the armed forces in 2025, which allowed more active officers to serve in civilian posts.

## Geography

Indonesia is an equatorial archipelago of coastal plains, mountains, and volcanic islands. The country lies between latitudes 11°S and 6°N and longitudes 95°E and 141°E. It is the world's largest country made up only of islands, extending 5,120 kilometres (3,181 mi) from east to west across three time zones and 1,760 kilometres (1,094 mi) from north to south. Indonesia shares land borders with Papua New Guinea, Timor-Leste, and Malaysia, as well as maritime borders with seven other countries, including Australia, Singapore, and the Philippines. Several straits in the archipelago, including Malacca, Sunda, and Lombok, are strategic shipping routes between the Indian and Pacific oceans.

Indonesia's archipelagic and territorial waters cover more area than its land, and its exclusive economic zone (EEZ) gives it rights over marine resources farther offshore. The country is recognised as an archipelagic state under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, allowing it to draw straight baselines connecting its outermost islands and exercise sovereignty over the enclosed waters, subject to the navigational rights of other countries. Indonesia has designated three north–south archipelagic sea-lane systems for international navigation through the waters between its islands, while passage rights continue along other normally used routes.

The number of Indonesian islands varies by counting method, with published figures ranging from 13,000 to as high as 25,000. The official count in 2025 places the total at 17,380 islands, with only 9.8% of them inhabited. Its five main islands are Sumatra, Java, Sulawesi, Borneo, and New Guinea. Indonesia occupies most of Borneo, known locally as Kalimantan, and shares the island with Brunei and the eastern part of Malaysia. It also occupies the western part of New Guinea, commonly called Papua, while Papua New Guinea occupies the eastern part.

Java occupies less than 7% of Indonesia's land area but is its most populous island, with intensive farming and extensive urban growth. Forest cover differs considerably, with larger forested areas in Papua and Maluku than in Java and Bali. At 4,884 metres (16,024 ft), Puncak Jaya on New Guinea is Indonesia's highest peak, while Lake Toba on Sumatra is its largest lake. In Kalimantan, major waterways like the Kapuas, Barito, and Mahakam rivers have historically served as corridors for movement and trade between communities in the interior and those near the coast.

Indonesia lies along the boundaries of the Eurasian, Indo-Australian, and Pacific-Philippine Sea tectonic plates. In some areas, one plate moves beneath another, a process known as subduction that causes frequent earthquakes and volcanic activity. The resulting volcanic belt contains at least 127 active volcanoes. This tectonic setting exposes the archipelago to major earthquake and tsunami hazards. The Sumatra earthquake in 2004 generated a tsunami that caused widespread destruction in Aceh. The Toba super-eruption about 74,000 years ago is one of the largest known eruptions in Earth's history, and the 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora was followed by global cooling and unusually cold weather in Europe and northeastern North America during the Year Without a Summer in 1816.

### Climate

Indonesia's equatorial position and monsoon winds give it a tropical climate. Most lowland areas are warm and humid throughout the year, while higher terrain is cooler, and seasonal temperature differences are small. Seasonal changes in the monsoon generally bring wetter conditions from November to April and drier conditions from May to October, but their timing and intensity vary by region. Much of the country has a tropical rainforest climate, with tropical monsoon and savanna climates occurring where dry seasons are more pronounced. Seasonal rainfall affects rice production and the risk of droughts, floods, and fires.

Western Sumatra, western Java, and portions of Kalimantan and Papua receive the highest annual rainfall, whereas Nusa Tenggara, the island group east of Java and closer to Australia, experiences a much drier climate. The surrounding oceans, island geography, monsoon winds, and topography all affect where and when rain falls. In drought-prone regions of the country, reduced rainfall and prolonged dry periods can cause water shortages and crop losses, particularly during El Niño, a recurring period of unusually warm waters in the tropical Pacific.

Indonesia already faces climate-related hazards. Climate projections indicate further warming and sea-level rise, changes in rainfall patterns, and greater risks from extreme heat and flooding. These changes affect agriculture, water resources, public health, coastal settlements and wildfire management. Sea-level rise is especially relevant to coastal areas, where much of the population and infrastructure are located. The country is among the largest emitters of greenhouse gases. It has committed to achieving net-zero greenhouse gas emissions by 2060, but coal still supplies most of its electricity despite growth in renewable generation.

### Biodiversity and conservation

Indonesia is among the 17 countries identified as megadiverse by Conservation International, a classification for countries with exceptionally high biodiversity. Its many islands and geological history have helped produce a diverse flora and fauna. The islands of Wallacea, including Sulawesi, the Maluku Islands and much of Nusa Tenggara, lie between the Asian and Australian faunal regions and support many species found nowhere else. In 2015, an estimated 83% of Southeast Asia's old-growth forest was in Indonesia. Mangrove forests, coral reefs and seagrass beds make up much of the coastal ecosystems.

Large oil palm and timber plantations were estimated to account for more than 40% of national deforestation between 2001 and 2016. Conversion of forest land for farming, roads, and other development breaks up remaining habitat, which affects species populations, habitat quality, and biodiversity. Carbon-rich peatlands are vulnerable to fires after drainage, especially during droughts. Forest and peatland fires produce haze that affects public health in Indonesia and neighbouring countries, including Malaysia and Singapore. At sea, coral diversity has been found to be lower on reefs damaged by pollution or anchors. A study of 32 locations found that the area covered by living coral was largely stable at most, but some reefs exposed to severe ocean heat suffered substantial losses.

Indonesia has designated 554 protected areas covering 27 million hectares, equivalent to 14% of its land area. Assessments conducted in 2015, 2017 and 2019 found improvements in management scores but identified poaching, illegal logging, human settlements, tourism and non-timber cultivation as the main threats to protected areas. The country's social forestry programme aims to give Indigenous and other local communities greater rights to manage their forests, but complex procedures and implementation problems have limited participation.

## Government and politics

Indonesia is a unitary republic with a presidential system under the 1945 Constitution. Following the fall of Suharto in 1998, four constitutional amendments between 1999 and 2002 introduced direct presidential elections, established new state institutions and reduced the powers of the People's Consultative Assembly (MPR). The amendments also gave regional autonomy a constitutional basis, while decentralisation laws granted provincial, regency and city governments greater authority to make local regulations.

The president serves as head of state and government, commands the armed forces, and may serve no more than two five-year terms. The MPR comprises elected members of the House of Representatives (DPR) and the Regional Representative Council (DPD). The DPR makes laws jointly with the president and exercises budgetary and oversight powers, while the DPD represents the provinces and may propose and discuss legislation concerning regional affairs but cannot give final approval to laws. The MPR can amend the constitution, inaugurate the president and vice-president, and remove them through constitutional procedures.

Indonesia's legal system is based largely on civil law inherited from Dutch rule, alongside customary (adat) and Islamic legal traditions. Judicial power is exercised by the Supreme Court and its subordinate general, religious, military and administrative courts, alongside the Constitutional Court. The Supreme Court hears final appeals and reviews regulations below the level of statutes. The Constitutional Court reviews laws for constitutionality and adjudicates disputes over state powers, political parties and election results. It also determines whether allegations against the president or vice-president meet the constitutional grounds for impeachment. The Religious Courts hear cases involving marriage, divorce, inheritance and certain transactions governed by Islamic law. The Judicial Commission proposes candidates for the Supreme Court and oversees judicial conduct.

### Parties and elections

Since the return to competitive elections in 1999, Indonesia has had a multi-party system. No party has won a majority in legislative elections, and presidents have generally formed broad governing coalitions, often appointing ministers from parties beyond their own.

Political parties have disagreed over the role of Islam in politics and its relationship with the state, with some rooted in secular-nationalist movements and others in Islamic political traditions. Party politics often relies on patronage and elite power-sharing. Parties that oppose a winning presidential candidate have sometimes joined the government afterward, often gaining cabinet positions and access to state resources through these alliances.

Indonesia held its first general election in 1955. Direct presidential elections began in 2004. General elections are held every five years, with political parties contesting DPR seats and individuals standing for the DPD. The independent General Elections Commission organises general elections, a task that includes delivering ballots and equipment to remote islands, mountainous regions and places with limited transport infrastructure.

### Administrative divisions

Indonesia is divided into 38 provinces, which are further divided into regencies and cities. Under the general system, these levels are headed by governors, regents and mayors, respectively, and have elected regional legislatures. Regencies and cities are divided into districts, followed by villages (desa) or urban villages (kelurahan). Villages exercise local self-government, whereas urban villages form part of the district administration.

Decentralisation after 1998 gave provincial, regency and city governments greater authority over local affairs. Special autonomy arrangements apply to Aceh, which may implement aspects of Islamic law, and to Papua. The Special Region of Yogyakarta retains a hereditary sultan as governor, while Jakarta has special provincial autonomy as the national capital. In 2022, Nusantara in East Kalimantan was designated as the future national capital. Its formal transfer from Jakarta requires a presidential decree.

### Foreign relations and military

Indonesia follows an "independent and active" (bebas aktif) foreign policy, a doctrine formulated by Vice President Mohammad Hatta in 1948. The policy has emphasised non-alignment and independent action, along with a preference for diplomacy over military means in pursuing regional objectives. One early expression of this policy was promoting decolonisation with the hosting of the Bandung Conference in 1955, the precursor of the Non-Aligned Movement (NAM). As a middle power, Indonesia works through regional and multilateral institutions while preserving room to manoeuvre in its relations with major powers.

As a founding member of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN), Indonesia places the organisation at the centre of its regional diplomacy. Indonesia has broad bilateral ties with Malaysia and Singapore, including cooperation in economic, defence, and cultural fields. Relations with Australia are conducted through a Comprehensive Strategic Partnership covering several areas, including economic development and regional stability. On China–United States competition, Indonesia has avoided alliances and taking sides, emphasised an ASEAN-led regional approach and sought to limit escalation.

Indonesia has been a member of the United Nations since 1950, apart from an absence in 1965–66 during a conflict with Malaysia. It participates in multilateral forums including the NAM, G20, the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation, and the East Asia Summit. After decades as a major recipient of foreign aid, Indonesia has been providing development assistance since 2015 and established Indonesian AID in 2019 to manage overseas assistance. Since 1957, it has sent military and police personnel to UN peacekeeping missions.

The Indonesian National Armed Forces comprise the Army, Navy, and Air Force. The Navy patrols Indonesian waters and strategic shipping routes and transports forces between islands, while the Air Force undertakes air defence, reconnaissance and transport operations. The Army is the largest branch and maintains regional commands paralleling civilian administration down to the village level. Its longstanding dominance is rooted in the military's historical emphasis on internal security. The "dual function" (dwifungsi) doctrine, which gave the military political and security roles, was formally ended in 2000, and its unelected parliamentary seats were abolished by 2004. Parliament's cooperative oversight has stabilised civil–military relations, but has also hindered further reform and weakened scrutiny of defence policy.

### Law enforcement and human rights

Law enforcement is carried out by the Indonesian National Police, whose duties include maintaining public security and order, enforcing the law, and providing protection and services to the public. The Chief of the Indonesian National Police is directly responsible to the president.

The National Commission on Human Rights (Komnas HAM) is authorised to investigate human rights violations. Accountability for past abuses has been limited. In 2024, the United Nations Human Rights Committee noted that only four of 16 cases of gross human rights violations investigated by Komnas HAM had reached court. Indonesia retains the death penalty but has not executed anyone since 2016.

Anti-Chinese racism has persisted despite the removal of most discriminatory policies after Suharto's New Order. Since 2016, anti-LGBTQ campaigns have included hostile statements by public officials, police raids and proposals for criminal restrictions. Religious practices of minority communities have also been limited by the blasphemy law. In Papua, human rights monitors have reported extrajudicial killings and disappearances, restrictions on peaceful expression, and displacement due to security operations and armed conflict.

## Economy

Indonesia has a mixed economy in which both the private sector and the government play major roles. In 2025, gross domestic product (GDP) was Rp23.821 quadrillion (US$1.45 trillion) at current prices, making it Southeast Asia's largest economy and placing it among the world's top 20 by nominal GDP and top 10 by GDP at purchasing power parity. Services and industry make up the largest shares of GDP, while agriculture is the largest single sector by employment. The country's currency is the rupiah.

The economy was once largely agricultural, and early post-independence policy focused on agricultural self-sufficiency. Industrialisation and urbanisation sped up from the late 1960s, with falling oil prices in the 1980s prompting a shift towards manufactured exports. From the mid-1980s to the mid-1990s, trade barriers fell and export-oriented manufacturing expanded as economic growth continued and poverty declined. The Asian financial crisis of 1997–98 caused a severe contraction and exposed weaknesses in the banking system. Reforms strengthened banking oversight and government finances, while allowing the rupiah's exchange rate to fluctuate more freely. These changes helped the economy withstand later financial shocks, including the global financial crisis in 2008. Following the COVID-19 pandemic, exports and public and private consumption helped restore economic growth.

In 2024, micro, small and medium-sized enterprises generate more than 60% of national GDP and employ more than 95% of the total workforce. Private consumption is also a major driver of growth. Informal employment is widespread, including in agriculture, retail, and transport services. Informal firms tend to have lower productivity and pay, and workers outside larger firms have more limited access to social insurance. Businesses also face corruption, burdensome and sometimes conflicting local regulations, and infrastructure issues such as power outages and road congestion. Women's labour force participation has remained lower than men's since the 1990s.

The official poverty rate was about 8% in March 2026. Expenditure inequality, measured by the Gini coefficient, stood at 0.368 in the same month and was higher in urban than in rural areas. The social protection system primarily supports low-income households. Mechanisms include food assistance and cash transfers based on several requirements, such as access to certain health or education services.

### Economic geography and sectors

The country's many islands affect where economic activity takes place and how goods move across the country. Economic activity is concentrated on Java, with Jakarta in particular contributing a disproportionate share of national output. Meanwhile, much of eastern Indonesia has weaker infrastructure and comparatively little manufacturing. High domestic transport and logistics costs constrain economic activity outside Java and make it harder to connect producers in the eastern regions with major markets.

Agricultural production includes rice and palm oil, two commodities for which the country ranks among the world's largest producers. Rice is the country's main staple food, while palm oil production continues to expand due to rising global demand. Palm oil made up much of Indonesia's agricultural and food exports in 2024. Fishing and aquaculture support millions of jobs, particularly in coastal communities. Indonesia is among the largest producers in marine capture fisheries, with about 9% of global output in 2024.

Major industries in manufacturing include food and beverage processing, chemicals, textiles and clothing, motor vehicles and electronics. Food and beverage processing is the largest industry, contributing 35.1% of manufacturing value added. Exports of manufactured goods include jewellery, electrical equipment, machinery and chemical products. Indonesia participates less in cross-border production and uses fewer imported inputs in its exports, compared with other ASEAN economies.

Tourism is a major service industry, with domestic visitor spending reaching about US$170 billion in 2023. The industry employs millions in businesses such as accommodation, food services, transport, and tourism goods retail. The country's digital economy is the largest in Southeast Asia, with about US$90 billion in transactions in 2024, primarily from e-commerce. Digital services have widened opportunities for small businesses.

Natural resource industries affect both investment and economic growth, which can rise or fall with commodity prices. Policies since 2020 have promoted domestic nickel refining by restricting exports of unprocessed ore. Major exports include palm oil, coal, ferronickel and liquefied natural gas. Most export destinations are in Asia, but the United States and the European Union are also major markets. Indonesia participates in regional economic cooperation through ASEAN and APEC and maintains trade and investment ties with the wider global economy, but its exports and imports make up a smaller share of GDP than in most other ASEAN economies.

### Infrastructure

Infrastructure connects Indonesia's dispersed islands and supports trade, access to services and economic activity. The archipelagic geography has made infrastructure development costly and complicated, particularly in transport and logistics. The Palapa Ring network of land and undersea fibre-optic cables has expanded internet access, though some remote areas remain underserved.

Shipping and domestic air services connect islands, including remote and outermost regions. Road transport dominates passenger and freight movement, and reliance on personal vehicles regularly causes traffic congestion in cities where public transport is limited. Rail transport is concentrated on Java, but the Makassar–Parepare railway has brought rail services to Sulawesi. The Jakarta–Bandung high-speed railway, operating since 2023, is the first high-speed railway in Southeast Asia.

Indonesia produced nearly twice as much primary energy as it consumed in 2023, with coal dominating both primary energy production and electricity generation. The state-owned electricity company controls transmission and distribution networks and purchases electricity from independent power producers for supply to the national grids. Electricity reaches nearly all households, but some remote communities face unreliable service or lack grid connections. Indonesia ranked first globally in geothermal generation, although renewables supplied only a small share of electricity in 2023.

## Demographics

Civil registration data in 2026 put Indonesia's population at 290.1 million, an increase from 270.2 million recorded in the 2020 Indonesian census. The country is the world's fourth most populous after India, China and the United States. The population is concentrated on Java and in urban centres, whereas many provinces outside Java and Bali are much less densely populated. In 2025, Java was home to 55.6% of the country's population, and all six of its provinces had population densities above the national average of 150 people per square kilometre (390 people/mi2).

Median age was 30.7 years in 2026, while about 69% of the population was aged 15–64 in 2025. A 2025 population survey recorded a total fertility rate of 2.13 births per woman, which is close to the level needed to replace each generation. Meanwhile, almost 12% of the population was aged 60 or older.

Approximately 59% of Indonesians lived in urban areas as of 2025. About 69% of urban households in the same year had access to adequate housing, based on national standards for living space, construction durability, drinking water and sanitation. Under the United Nations' urban-area definition, Jakarta was the world's most populous city in 2025, with nearly 42 million inhabitants. Urbanisation has resulted from migration from rural areas, births exceeding deaths in cities, and the reclassification of rural settlements as urban. It has also expanded metropolitan regions, especially on Java, and increased demand for infrastructure, basic services, and city management.

About 8 million Indonesians reside abroad according to a 2017 estimate, with large communities in Malaysia, the Netherlands, Saudi Arabia, Singapore, and Taiwan. Many of them work in domestic service and other low-skilled jobs.

### Ethnic groups and languages

The 2010 census listed over 1,300 ethnic and sub-ethnic categories. A later study in 2015 reclassified them into about 600 groups after noting classification difficulties. Austronesian-speaking populations predominate in much of western and central Indonesia. Melanesian populations live mainly in the eastern islands, including parts of Nusa Tenggara, Maluku, and Papua. The Javanese are the largest ethnic group, making up about 40% of the population. Other major groups include the Sundanese, Malay, Batak, Madurese, Betawi, Minangkabau, and Bugis.

The country's official language, Indonesian, is a standardised form of Malay. Malay had long served as the archipelago's lingua franca before the 1928 Youth Pledge declared Indonesian the language of national unity. It became the official language upon Indonesian independence in 1945. Written in the Latin script, Indonesian has spread through education, government, and mass media, and serves as a common language across ethnic and regional boundaries.

More than 700 languages are spoken across the archipelago. These fall broadly into the Austronesian family and several unrelated language families collectively called Papuan. Javanese is the most widely spoken local language and has official regional status in Yogyakarta. Several regional languages have their own traditional writing systems, some of which are now used only to a limited extent. Local languages remain part of regional identity and cultural heritage, even as Indonesian dominates national public life. Dutch never became widely spoken in Indonesia, but some Dutch-language civil and commercial codes have continued to apply.

### Religion

Indonesia recognises six religions: Islam, Protestantism, Roman Catholicism, Hinduism, Buddhism, and Confucianism, while the constitution guarantees freedom of religion. The state regulates religious affiliation, marriages, and places of worship. Indigenous beliefs have gained separate legal recognition, but their followers still face discrimination. Pancasila makes belief in one God one of the state ideology's five principles and is used to support national unity and religious harmony. Religious minorities still face discrimination, including through local religious bylaws, social pressure, and restrictions on worship.

Indonesia has the largest Muslim population in the world, with 251 million Muslims in 2025, or 87.15% of the population. Most Indonesian Muslims are Sunni, with the Shafi'i school of Islamic law historically dominant. The two largest Muslim organisations in the country, Nahdlatul Ulama (NU) and Muhammadiyah, represent two major strands of Islam in Indonesia. NU is rooted in traditionalist Islam, which emphasises preserving established Islamic scholarly traditions and religious practices, while Muhammadiyah is associated with reformist teachings, which emphasise interpreting Islamic scriptures directly. Both also act as social institutions and are active in civic affairs. They have also taken part in debates over religious teaching, public policy and the place of Islam in Indonesian society.

Christians are the country's second-largest religious group, numbering 30.1 million people, or 10.44% of the population. Some provinces in eastern Indonesia are Christian-majority, including the East Nusa Tenggara province and much of Papua. Hindus (1.66%, or 4.7 million people) are concentrated in Bali, while Buddhists (0.69%, or 1.9 million people) are found mainly among Chinese Indonesians, though not exclusively.

Many local belief systems predate major world religions and continue alongside them. They include beliefs in ancestors, spirits, and powers associated with nature. Examples include Sunda Wiwitan in West Java, Javanese Kejawèn, and Dayak Kaharingan, practised within or alongside recognised religions. In Bali, Hinduism developed through interaction with local religious traditions and practices. Local customs have shaped Islamic practices across Indonesia, sometimes causing disagreements over religious orthodoxy between traditionalists and reformists.

### Education

Indonesia's education system includes more than 50 million students, over 3 million teachers and over 400,000 educational institutions. It is administered by ministries responsible for school education, higher education and religious education, and generally follows a 6-3-3-4 structure: six years of primary school, three each of junior and senior secondary school, and typically four for an undergraduate degree.

National curricula promote unity and national identity through Pancasila education and lessons on the country's cultural and religious diversity. Access to education and school quality vary, particularly between urban and rural areas and among regions with differing levels of development. Teacher quality and distribution are also persistent issues. Student learning outcomes are low by OECD benchmarks. In PISA 2022, Indonesian 15-year-olds performed below OECD averages in mathematics, reading and science, with most not reaching the minimum proficiency level in each subject.

Government expenditure on education was approximately 2.4% of GDP in 2024. In 2022, Indonesia had 4,481 higher education institutions, including universities, Islamic colleges, institutions preparing students for government service, and an open university. The University of Indonesia, Gadjah Mada University, and the Bandung Institute of Technology are among the highest-ranked public universities in international rankings. Higher education has expanded since 1990 and helps develop workforce skills, but access, participation, and institutional quality are uneven.

### Healthcare

Primary care is provided through community health centres (puskesmas), private clinics and general practitioners. In 2014, Indonesia launched National Health Insurance (Jaminan Kesehatan Nasional), one of the world's largest single-payer health-insurance systems, covering 98% of the population by 2024. Despite near-universal coverage, access to healthcare and the availability of medical personnel still vary by location. Government expenditure on healthcare was approximately 1.4% of GDP in 2024.

Life expectancy increased between 1990 and 2019 from 62.5 to 69.4 years for men and from 65.7 to 73.5 years for women, but there are substantial differences between provinces. Non-communicable diseases, including heart disease and diabetes, account for a growing share of health loss, while infectious diseases such as tuberculosis continue to cause illness and premature death. Stunting prevalence among children under five was 22% in 2024.

## Culture

Cultures in Indonesia differ across islands, languages, and communities. Earlier populations and later Austronesian migrations contributed to this diversity, with Melanesian communities prominent in parts of eastern Indonesia. Movement by sea placed the archipelago within trading and migration networks involving Indian, Arab, Persian, Chinese, and mainland Southeast Asian peoples, followed later by Europeans. Religious and courtly influences entered through these routes, and European expansion ultimately brought colonial domination. Coastal commercial centres often encountered outside influences before they reached inland communities.

Indonesia has no single, uniform cultural tradition. Regional traditions draw on local languages and cultures, while forms intended for audiences across Indonesia generally use the Indonesian language. These traditions include performance, visual art, ritual, clothing, food, and literature. Modern popular culture includes cinema, television drama, music, celebrity media and reality shows, along with imported Asian dramas and global formats adapted for Indonesian audiences.

Indonesia has 16 items on the UNESCO intangible heritage lists, including wayang puppet theatre, batik, angklung, the saman dance, and pencak silat. Other entries include pantun, kebaya, and kolintang, which were inscribed through multinational nominations.

### National identity and observances

Indonesia's national symbols include the red-and-white flag, the Garuda Pancasila emblem and the national anthem, Indonesia Raya. The scroll on the Garuda emblem carries an Old Javanese motto, Bhinneka Tunggal Ika, usually translated as "Unity in Diversity". The phrase comes from Sutasoma, a 14th-century Majapahit poem by Mpu Tantular expressing the unity of Hindu and Buddhist traditions. In its modern national usage, it expresses the ideal of unity among Indonesia's different ethnic, linguistic and religious communities.

Pancasila is the country's foundational philosophy. It comprises five principles: belief in one God, just and civilised humanity, national unity, democracy through deliberation and representation, and social justice. They appear in the preamble to the 1945 Constitution and have formed part of official efforts to define Indonesian national identity. Other prominent social concepts include gotong royong (mutual assistance) and musyawarah (deliberation and consensus). Adat encompasses customary norms and traditions that vary among communities.

National holidays include Independence Day on 17 August and religious observances such as Eid al-Fitr, Christmas, Nyepi, Vesak and Chinese New Year. Independence Day is commemorated through flag-raising ceremonies, community celebrations, and traditional competitions. Eid al-Fitr, known locally as Lebaran, follows Ramadan and is associated with mudik (homecoming travel), family visits and requests for forgiveness. In Bali, Nyepi marks the Hindu Saka New Year with silence and restrictions on work and travel.

### Visual arts and architecture

Indonesian arts include textiles, puppetry, masks, carving, and painting. Textiles are used in rituals and can convey social standing and ethnic affiliation. Colonial rule and nationalism affected the development of modern painting, and tourism and urban art communities became increasingly important later in the 20th century. Regional painting traditions include Bali's classical Kamasan style, whose narrative scenes share figures with wayang puppet theatre. Sculptural traditions include megalithic monuments, Ngaju Dayak hampatong figures used for commemoration or protection, and Asmat carvings with spiritual and aesthetic roles.

Indonesian architecture ranges from ordinary dwellings to traditional houses (rumah adat), whose designs and rituals express ancestry, status and local beliefs. Regional forms include the Javanese pendapa pavilions, Dayak longhouses, Minangkabau Rumah Gadang, and Toraja Tongkonan clan houses. Monumental religious architecture includes Borobudur and Prambanan, while surviving Hindu and Buddhist sculpture from the 8th to 15th centuries includes works in stone, bronze, and gold.

Clothing traditions differ by region and are used in ceremonies, weddings, formal occasions, and as markers of local identity. Batik and kebaya are widely used in national and formal dress and are especially prominent in Javanese culture. Other regional textile traditions include Minangkabau gold-thread textiles and ikat (yarn dyed in patterns before weaving) among Batak and many eastern Indonesian communities. These textiles are used at formal events, in bridal exchanges and during ceremonies and weddings.

### Performing arts

Indonesian music and dance include court, village, ritual, and popular forms. Gamelan orchestras are especially prominent in Java and Bali, while angklung, made from shaken bamboo tubes, and other bamboo ensembles are found in western Java and Banyumas. Drums, gongs, lutes and singing often accompany dance. Islamic-influenced music includes lute-based gambus and devotional qasida songs. Popular genres include string-based keroncong and dangdut, which draws on Malay, Indian, Arabic and Western pop and rock influences.

Mask dances appear in Javanese court drama and Balinese ritual performance, while Dayak mask dances are performed in agricultural rituals and communal ceremonial settings. Javanese court dance includes forms such as bedhaya and serimpi, while other local dances are associated with theatre and music, spirit possession, harvests, temple festivals, cremation ceremonies, and commemorations of the dead. Contemporary dance groups also adapt K-pop choreography and hip-hop.

Traditional Indonesian theatre encompasses storytelling, music, movement, puppetry, masks, acting, and dance. Wayang is a prominent puppet-theatre tradition in Java that is also practised in Bali. A dalang (puppeteer) leads performances accompanied by gamelan, often drawing stories from the Indian epics Ramayana and Mahabharata. It has served ritual, moral, comic, political, and public-messaging functions. Other regional traditions combine drama with music, dance, masks, or martial arts, and modern theatre groups have used their performances for social and political commentary.

### Literature

Literary traditions developed in many regional languages and included both oral and written forms. Oral traditions ranged from storytelling to songs, customs, beliefs, and other community forms, and material could move between oral and written forms. Traditional written literature includes court chronicles, religious and legal writing, narrative prose, and verse. These forms often served ethical, religious, or dynastic purposes rather than entertainment.

Modern literature grew in the early 20th century alongside the expansion of literacy, publishing, and a wider reading public. Balai Pustaka, originating from a Dutch colonial commission established in 1908 and named in 1917, distributed books through libraries and agencies across the archipelago, giving writers a wider readership. During this period, prose fiction and poetry became more prominent, displacing some older literary forms. From 1933 until the Japanese occupation in 1942, the literary magazine Poedjangga Baroe published essays, poetry, short stories, and a novel. Newspapers, magazines, and other publications carried Indonesian-language prose and poetry during the prewar period.

In the 1970s and 1980s, newspapers and magazines were important outlets, with regular sections carrying poetry, short stories, criticism, and serial fiction. From the late 1990s, some forms of literary activity began to emerge in digital media, as websites, mailing lists, forums and blogs provided spaces for literary communities and publication. After Suharto's government fell in 1998, writers faced fewer state restrictions and explored politics and sexuality more openly, alongside works addressing Islamic themes.

### Cinema and mass media

Feature film production began during the Dutch colonial era with Loetoeng Kasaroeng (1926), based on a Sundanese legend, and a small number of new film companies emerged after independence. Films became vehicles for nationalist and anti-imperialist politics under President Sukarno, while Suharto's New Order government later regulated and censored cinema. The domestic film industry declined sharply in the early 1990s. After the fall of Suharto in 1998, independent filmmakers, film communities, festivals, and renewed commercial production helped revive Indonesian cinema. Nevertheless, state censorship and pressure from some Islamic groups continued to restrict filmmakers' choices.

During the New Order, newspapers and broadcasters operated under licensing and censorship, and television was used to advance state-led development and a unified vision of national identity. During the reforms after 1998, the Press Law of 1999 prohibited censorship and publication bans and removed publishing-licence requirements, but journalists and news organisations continued to face lawsuits, intimidation and physical attacks, sometimes involving government officials.

Internet use grew rapidly from the mid-1990s, and websites provided another means of distributing news and information. Mainstream news organisations increasingly use the same reporting across television, print and online outlets, either through integrated newsrooms or by sharing content between them. News is also shortened or reformatted for websites and social media.

### Cuisine

Indonesian cuisine differs from one region to another in its ingredients, methods, and flavours. Trade and migration brought Indian, Chinese and Middle Eastern influences over centuries, and Portuguese and other European traders arriving from the 16th century, followed by Dutch colonial rule, introduced further ingredients and cooking practices. Indonesian cuisine also spread abroad, particularly to the Netherlands, where cookbooks and migration introduced Indonesian food to Dutch society by the late 19th century.

Rice is the staple food in much of the country and is commonly served with meat or fish, vegetables, sambal (a chilli-based condiment), and other accompaniments. Chilli, coconut milk, shrimp paste, peanuts, garlic, shallots, and tamarind are widely used in cooking, along with foods such as chicken and seafood. Peanut sauce commonly accompanies gado-gado, a vegetable salad, and sate, grilled skewered meat.

Tempeh, fermented soybeans originating in Java, and tofu (tahu) are widely consumed soybean products across Indonesia. Noodles (mie) and fried rice (nasi goreng) are also popular. West Sumatra's Minangkabau cuisine includes rendang, beef slowly cooked with spices and coconut milk. Tumpeng is a Javanese dish of cone-shaped rice and side dishes, served at life-cycle and communal ceremonies to express gratitude and seek blessings.

### Sports

Popular sports in Indonesia include football, badminton, weightlifting, and basketball, along with local games and martial arts. Football is one of the most followed sports, with active club supporters and public screenings of matches. The team representing colonial Indonesia was the first Asian representative to appear at the FIFA World Cup, taking part in the 1938 tournament.

Indonesia has been particularly successful in international badminton, winning both the Thomas and Uber Cups, the men's and women's world team championships. Most of Indonesia's Olympic medals have come from badminton and weightlifting. Indonesia first competed at the Olympics in 1952 and has participated in the Asian Games since their inaugural edition in 1951. Jakarta has hosted the Asian Games twice, first in 1962 and again in 2018 with Palembang.

Traditional sports and games are featured in ceremonies, harvest events, contests, and public entertainment. Examples include sepak takraw, played by kicking a woven ball, bull racing (karapan sapi) in Madura, whip fighting (caci) in Flores, and horseback spear contests (pasola) in Sumba. Pencak silat is a form of Indonesian martial arts and was included as an official event at the 2018 Asian Games, where Indonesia won 14 gold medals in the sport.
