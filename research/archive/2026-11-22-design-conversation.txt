Case-based reasoning

Case-Based Reasoning (CBR) is an artificial intelligence and cognitive science problem-solving paradigm that solves new problems by retrieving, adapting, and reusing solutions from similar past problems ("cases"). Rather than relying solely on generalized rules or statistical training weights, CBR reasons directly from specific historical experiences.

The 4-Step CBR Cycle (The "4 Rs")
CBR operates through a continuous learning loop known as the Aamodt & Plaza cycle:

       [ New Problem ]
              │
              ▼
      1. RETRIEVE ──────► [ Case Base / Memory ]
              │                     ▲
              ▼                     │
         2. REUSE                   │
              │                     │
              ▼                     │
         3. REVISE              4. RETAIN
              │                     │
              ▼                     │
    [ Verified Solution ] ──────────┘
Retrieve: Given a new target problem, search the memory repository (the case base) to find the most similar past cases using similarity metrics (such as nearest-neighbor algorithms or feature weighting).

Reuse: Map the solution from the retrieved case(s) to the target problem. If the new problem matches closely, the past solution may be applied directly; otherwise, it requires adaptation to fit the new constraints.

Revise: Test and evaluate the proposed solution in the real world or a simulation. If it fails or falls short, repair and refine the solution based on the feedback.

Retain: Store the newly solved problem and its verified outcome as a new case in the case base, allowing the system to learn incrementally from experience without retraining from scratch.

Core Components of a Case
A typical case in a CBR system is structured into three parts:

Problem Description: The state of the world, symptoms, or input parameters when the case occurred.

Solution: The specific action taken, diagnosis made, or plan executed to resolve the problem.

Outcome (Optional): The resulting state of the world after applying the solution, indicating whether it succeeded or failed.

CBR vs. Rule-Based Systems vs. Machine Learning
Feature	Case-Based Reasoning (CBR)	Rule-Based Systems	Deep / Statistical ML
Knowledge Unit	Specific historical instances (cases)	Explicit IF-THEN logic rules	Learned weights / statistical patterns
Learning Mechanism	Incremental (adds new cases to memory)	Manual rule authoring by experts	Batch training over large datasets
Explainability	High ("Solved this way because of Case #42")	High (traces fired rules)	Low to moderate (often a "black box")
Data Requirement	Works well with small, sparse datasets	Requires complete domain formalization	Requires large, representative datasets
Best Suited For	Ill-structured domains with exceptions	Well-defined, deterministic workflows	High-dimensional perception & prediction
Real-World Applications
Medical Diagnosis & Treatment: Comparing a patient's symptoms, lab results, and medical history against prior patient records to suggest diagnoses or personalized treatment plans.

Customer Support & Help Desks: Matching incoming support tickets against resolved tickets to recommend troubleshooting steps.

Legal Reasoning: Precedent-based legal systems (stare decisis), where judges and lawyers argue new cases by citing analogous past rulings.

Engineering & Design: Adapting existing architectural blueprints, mechanical parts, or software architectures to meet modified specifications.

LLM Agent Memory (Modern AI): In modern Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and agentic workflows, CBR is used to store past successful agent trajectories and tool-use traces so an LLM can retrieve and adapt prior strategies for new tasks.

Key Advantages and Limitations
Advantages: Avoids the "knowledge acquisition bottleneck" (no need to extract exhaustive rules from experts), learns continuously from single experiences, and provides natural, human-readable explanations via precedent.

Limitations: Performance degrades if the case base grows too large without indexing (the utility problem, where retrieval slows down), and case adaptation remains difficult to automate in complex domains.

can it learn from examples ?

Yes—learning from examples is the core mechanism of Case-Based Reasoning (CBR). In fact, each "case" is a concrete example from past experience: what the situation looked like, what was done, and what happened afterward.

However, CBR learns from examples differently than standard machine learning.

How CBR Learns from Examples
Single-example learning (no big dataset needed): A CBR system can learn from just one solved case right away. Once a problem is solved and verified in the Retain step, that single example is available to help solve similar problems next time.

Continuous, incremental updates: New examples are added to the case base over time without wiping out old knowledge or running expensive retraining cycles.

Learning from both successes and failures: Useful examples are not just wins. Recording a case where a solution failed or caused side effects helps the system avoid repeating that mistake in similar situations.

Refining solutions through use: When an old example does not fit a new problem exactly, adapting and testing it (Reuse and Revise) produces a better, updated example to store for the future.

CBR Examples vs. Machine Learning Examples
Aspect	Case-Based Reasoning	Standard Machine Learning
Role of an example	Kept as a specific reference case to compare against and adapt	Used to tune model weights or build general decision boundaries
Sample size	Works even with a handful of examples	Usually needs many examples to generalize reliably
What happens to edge cases	Kept as distinct cases so rare exceptions are not averaged away	Can get smoothed over or drowned out by majority patterns
A Quick Caveat
While CBR learns easily from examples, quality matters more than raw volume. If you feed it noisy, outdated, or conflicting examples without pruning or weighting them, retrieval can surface unhelpful cases or slow down as the case base grows.

in which format problems are solved ?

In Case-Based Reasoning (CBR), solutions are not forced into a single rigid file or data format—they take whatever structured or semi-structured representation the problem domain needs to execute or interpret the fix.

What stays consistent is the role of the solution inside a case: it captures what action, decision, or plan resolves the problem description.

Common Formats for CBR Solutions
Action steps or troubleshooting procedures: Ordered lists, workflows, or runbooks (for example, step-by-step IT diagnostics or customer support resolutions).

Parameter settings or configurations: Key-value pairs, JSON/YAML configs, or numerical adjustments (for example, updated controller gains, dosages, or pricing tiers).

Diagnoses or classifications: Labels, codes, or ranked hypotheses (for example, fault codes or medical conditions, often paired with a recommended next action).

Designs, plans, or artifacts: Blueprints, schedules, recipes, code snippets, or legal arguments adapted from a past template to fit new constraints.

Natural language or structured text: Prose explanations or semi-structured summaries—common in modern LLM-based CBR where past reasoning steps or tool trajectories are stored as text or structured logs.

Why Format Choice Matters
The format of a solution determines how easily CBR can adapt and verify it:

Numeric or key-value formats are easy to scale or tweak automatically (such as adjusting a quantity or threshold).

Structured plans or steps allow substituting or removing specific actions when constraints change.

Free text is flexible for human readers and LLMs, though harder to modify algorithmically without a model or rules.

0. Sizing check first: bandit vs. full MDP
One-shot "suggest a dish" = a contextual bandit: state → action → reward, episode over. No sequential structure.
Multi-turn (clarify → suggest → adapt → confirm, or a whole chat) = a full MDP — actually a POMDP, since the user's true pantry/intent is hidden and you only observe their messages.
Rule of thumb: if an action changes what you know or what options remain, you need an MDP. A clarifying question does exactly that — which is why even the cooking helper becomes a (tiny) MDP the moment you allow asking.

3. Chat agent: the real MDP
State ≈ your structured Problem Description from earlier + conversation context. The true intent is hidden → you act on a belief state, which the dialogue history approximates.
Reward is delayed and sparse: success arrives at the end ("that fixed it!"), so you need credit assignment — TD learning or return-to-go spreads the terminal reward back over the turns that caused it. Store per-turn rewards inside the trajectory case.
Transitions are the hard part: P is "what the user says next." You either learn model-free (skip P entirely, learn Q directly) or simulate users with an LLM to train against.

6. Practical caveats before you build this
Rewards are sparse and noisy — most users never say "5/5." Lean on implicit signals (follow-up questions, return visits, corrections) and consider a small learned reward model.
Users are non-stationary — preferences drift, so decay old case weights. That's just case-base maintenance wearing an RL hat.
Exploration costs real users — you can't ε-greedy bad recipes onto people. Use conservative/offline updates from logged feedback, or train against LLM-simulated users first.
Credit assignment across turns — a "thanks!" at turn 8 doesn't tell you which turn earned it; that's precisely what TD methods are for.
Bottom line
For your cooking helper, start with a contextual bandit with case-weighted priors — it's 20 lines on top of your existing script. Reserve the full MDP for the multi-turn chat agent. CBR supplies the memory and the prior; RL supplies the credit-assignment machinery that turns outcomes into better future decisions.

## Fixing "Too Many Open Files" (EMFILE) crashes in Node.js
When a Node.js microservice crashes with an EMFILE error, it means the operating system's file descriptor limit has been exhausted. First, check the current soft limit by running `ulimit -n` inside the container or on the host. If it returns 1024, that is far too low for a production server handling concurrent connections. Increase the system-wide limit by adding `fs.file-max = 65536` to `/etc/sysctl.conf` and applying it with `sysctl -p`. Additionally, audit your code for unclosed file streams, missing `res.end()` calls, or leaked database connection pool instances.

So the honest one-liner: we learn where a pattern applies and how much to trust it. We do not learn what it does.
When you hit "Promote to pattern," the anti-unification is real — the signature ask>grant is derived from observed behavior, and the intent grouping is discovered. But the skeleton it instantiates is looked up from a library of three I authored. That's template selection, not skeleton synthesis. I overclaimed when I said "keep the invariant sequence."
There's also a bug in it
Traces from cached moves get shape: "move". SKEL["move"] doesn't exist, so clone(undefined) throws inside the promote handler — the button silently does nothing.
You can trigger it: say hi, then how much is pro, then what time do you open Saturday. Three move-traces, signature direct, three distinct intents → a candidate appears → Promote throws. Even if it didn't throw, the resulting pattern would be inert, because recognize() requires atoms with conditions for non-lookup shapes, and there are no greeting/pricing/hours atoms.
Fix is two lines: skip shape === "move" candidates, and fall back to SKEL.gated on unknown shapes.
The deeper problem is circular
Look at where the shape label comes from:
js
S.traces.push({ intent: s.intent, shape: s.shape || "move", sig: s.sig || s.mode, ... })
s.shape is p.shape — the shape of the pattern that already ran. So induction can only ever re-group shapes that some existing pattern already implements. It can never observe a fourth shape, because nothing in the system can produce one.
That's a closed loop. Genuine skeleton discovery needs traces from outside the pattern loop — a human resolving something over several turns, where the move sequence wasn't generated by a skeleton at all.
What real skeleton learning would need
The crux is guard induction, and the current trace schema can't support it.
If you observe ask(packaging) → ask(days) → grant, you can anti-unify the op sequence easily. But to emit {op:"grant", guard:"gate_satisfied"} you must know that the gate was satisfied at that moment. Right now traces record the op and the intent — not the gate state, not slot boundness, not which condition flipped.
So the missing piece is concrete: log per-turn {unbound:[...], violated:[...], satisfied:bool} alongside the op. Then guards become inferable — "granted only when nothing was unbound and nothing violated" is gate_satisfied, derived rather than authored.
The askOrder bandit is much cheaper and the signal already exists: on a resolved conversation, whichever slot ordering reached a verdict in fewer turns gets its priority boosted. turnsLog plus per-sub verdicts is enough.
So: three things are honestly learned (grouping, trust, connectors), one is authored-and-selected (skeletons), and two are stored-but-dead (batch, proactive).
