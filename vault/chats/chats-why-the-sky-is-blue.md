Entry 1
U: My daughter asked me why the sky is blue, and I realised I did not know.
A: It is because of how sunlight scatters in the air. Blue light is scattered much more than red light.
U: So the blue comes from all directions, not only from the sun?
A: Yes, exactly. That is why the whole sky looks blue during the day.

Entry 2
U: Why is the sky blue and not violet, if violet scatters even more?
A: Good question. The sun gives off less violet light, and our eyes are less sensitive to it.
U: So it is partly about how we see?
A: Yes. The sky is a mix, and to our eyes that mix looks blue.

Entry 3
U: Why does the sky turn red at sunset?
A: At sunset the light passes through much more air. Most of the blue is scattered away before it reaches you.
U: And the red is what is left?
A: Exactly. Red and orange light get through more easily.

Entry 4
U: Is the sky blue on other planets?
A: Not always. On Mars the daytime sky looks more butterscotch, because of the dust.
U: I did not know that. What about the sunsets there?
A: They look bluish near the sun, which is the reverse of ours.

Entry 5
U: Why is the sky black at night?
A: Because the sun is no longer lighting the air above you. Without that light there is nothing to scatter.

Entry 6
U: Is the sea blue because it reflects the sky?
A: Only partly. Water itself absorbs red light, so deep water looks blue.
U: So it would be blue even under a grey sky?
A: Deep, clear water would still look bluish, though duller.

Entry 7
U: Why are clouds white if the sky is blue?
A: The droplets in clouds are much larger than air molecules. They scatter all colours about equally.
U: And all colours together look white?
A: Yes, that is right.

Entry 8
U: My son asked why the sky is blue and I said because it is. Was that wrong?
A: It is not much of an answer, but most parents have given it. Would you like a simple version?
U: Yes, please. He will certainly ask again.
A: You could say that sunlight is made of many colours, and the air bounces the blue around the most.

Entry 9
U: Who worked out why the sky is blue?
A: The explanation is usually credited to Lord Rayleigh in the nineteenth century. The effect is named after him.
U: Is that what people call Rayleigh scattering?
A: Yes, that is the one.

Entry 10
U: Is the sky blue in space?
A: No. Above the atmosphere there is no air to scatter the light, so the sky looks black.
U: Even with the sun shining?
A: Yes. Astronauts see a bright sun in a black sky.
